Vail Restaurants

Where mountain culture meets culinary excellence

Vail isn't just a ski town—it's become one of Colorado's premier dining destinations. The culinary scene here rivals resort towns anywhere in the world, with restaurants that have been drawing serious food lovers since the 1970s. From the slopes of Vail Mountain to the cobblestone streets of Vail Village, you'll find everything from white-tablecloth fine dining to casual spots where ski boots are welcome.

What makes Vail dining special is the combination of spectacular settings and genuinely talented chefs who've chosen mountain life. Many restaurants here have been family-owned for decades, while newer spots bring fresh creativity. Whether you're celebrating a powder day or planning a romantic evening, this guide covers the restaurants worth your time and money.

Fine Dining Worth the Splurge

Vail's fine dining scene punches well above its weight for a town this size. These restaurants require reservations—often weeks in advance during ski season—but deliver experiences you'll remember long after the trip ends.

Sweet Basil

Vail Village • Established 1977

The restaurant that put Vail dining on the map. For nearly 50 years, Sweet Basil has been serving New American cuisine that evolves with the seasons while maintaining the warmth that made it a local institution. The wine program is exceptional, and the patio offers prime people-watching on Bridge Street. Book well ahead for dinner; lunch is easier to snag.

Don't miss: The seasonal tasting menu showcases what the kitchen does best.

$$$$ Reservations Essential

Matsuhisa Vail

Vail Village • Chef Nobu Matsuhisa

Chef Nobu Matsuhisa's legendary Japanese-Peruvian fusion in an intimate Vail setting. The black cod miso is as good as any Nobu worldwide, but the cozy mountain atmosphere sets this location apart. It's expensive, but the omakase experience justifies the price for a special occasion. The bar is a good option if the dining room is booked.

Pro tip: The lunch menu offers the same quality at lower prices.

$$$$ Japanese-Peruvian

Mountain Standard

Vail Village • Modern American

A newer addition that's quickly become essential Vail dining. The concept is "refined mountain cuisine"—think elevated comfort food with Colorado ingredients. The rooftop bar is the real draw on sunny afternoons, offering craft cocktails and small plates with panoramic views. More accessible than the stuffier fine dining spots.

Best for: Groups who want quality without formality.

$$$ Rooftop Bar

Game Creek Restaurant

Mid-Mountain • Snowcat Access Only

The most unique dining experience in Vail. You reach this mountain lodge via snowcat or gondola—no driving. The setting alone justifies the trek: a European-style alpine lodge with floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the Gore Range. The cuisine is refined American with game meats and seasonal Colorado ingredients. Lunch is available; dinner is the main event.

Book ahead: Limited seating and high demand, especially for sunset dinner.

$$$$ Unique Experience

Casual Favorites & Local Hangouts

Not every meal needs to be a production. These spots are where locals actually eat—good food without pretense, often with better value than the tourist-focused places.

The Red Lion

Vail Village • Since 1966

The original Vail apres-ski institution. The Red Lion has been the first stop after last chair since the resort opened. It's loud, crowded, and unpretentious—exactly what apres should be. Live music most afternoons, cheap beer, and the kind of energy that makes you want to high-five strangers. The food is secondary to the atmosphere, but the burgers are solid.

When to go: 3-5pm for peak apres energy.

$$ Apres-Ski Legend

Loaded Joe's

West Vail • Breakfast & Coffee

Where locals fuel up before first chair. Loaded Joe's serves legitimately good coffee and massive breakfast burritos that'll power you through a full morning on the mountain. It's in West Vail, away from the village prices, with free parking. The vibe is casual—ski gear and wet boots welcome. Get there early on powder days; it gets crowded.

Order this: The "Loaded" breakfast burrito lives up to its name.

$ Early Morning

Pazzo's Pizzeria

Vail Village • Wood-Fired Pizza

Vail's best pizza, period. Pazzo's has been firing wood-oven pies in the village since 1991. The crust is thin and crispy, the ingredients are quality, and the casual atmosphere welcomes families and groups. It's one of the few village restaurants where you won't feel underdressed in ski clothes. Good craft beer selection too.

Try: The Bianca (white pizza) or any special with local ingredients.

$$ Family Friendly

On-Mountain Dining

Ski area food has a bad reputation, but Vail's on-mountain options are genuinely good. These spots offer more than cafeteria fare—some are destination-worthy restaurants that happen to be at 10,000 feet.

The 10th — At the top of Gondola One, this modern bistro serves elevated lunch fare with panoramic Gore Range views. The short rib tacos and truffle fries are standouts. No reservations needed, but arrive before noon or after 1pm to avoid lines.

Two Elk Lodge — Vail's largest on-mountain restaurant sits in spectacular China Bowl. It's technically cafeteria-style, but the carved meats and made-to-order stations are surprisingly good. The deck on a sunny day is hard to beat.

Mid-Vail — The most accessible mid-mountain spot with multiple food options. It's not gourmet, but it's convenient and the outdoor seating has excellent views.

Practical Tips for Dining in Vail

  • Reservations are essential — During ski season (December-March), book fine dining spots 2-4 weeks ahead. Even casual places fill up on weekends.
  • Prices are high — This is a luxury resort town. Budget $75-150 per person for fine dining, $30-50 for casual dinner. Lunch is more affordable.
  • Parking is tricky — Most village restaurants don't have parking. Use the structures and walk, or take the free bus from West Vail lodging.
  • Dress code varies — Fine dining expects resort casual (no ski boots). Casual spots welcome whatever you're wearing.
  • Summer is different — The scene is quieter, reservations easier, and some restaurants close. But the value is better and the weather is perfect for patios.
